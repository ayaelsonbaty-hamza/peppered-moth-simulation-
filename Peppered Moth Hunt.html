Natural selection, step by step

  1. VariationMoths in the population differ. Some are light, some are dark.
  2. Selection pressureBirds (you) eat the moths that are easiest to see.
  3. SurvivalMoths that are camouflaged on the bark are more likely to survive.
  4. ReproductionOnly survivors breed and have young.
  5. InheritanceYoung inherit their colour from their parent through their genes.

Repeat over many generations and the better-camouflaged colour becomes more common. The population changes. No single moth changes colour.

What really happened

  • 1848The first dark peppered moth is recorded in Manchester. Factory soot was killing pale lichen and blackening tree trunks.
  • 1895About 98% of peppered moths around Manchester are dark.
  • 1950sBernard Kettlewell releases and recaptures moths and shows birds eat more of the moths that stand out.
  • 1956The Clean Air Act cuts smoke. Lichen returns, and over the following decades the dark form becomes rare again.

Think like a scientist

Q1In the clean forest, which moths did you find first? Why?Show answer
The dark moths. They stand out against the pale, lichen-covered bark. Light moths are camouflaged, so they are harder to see and more of them survive.
Q2Switch to sooty bark and hunt for a few generations. What happens to the percentage of dark moths? Explain why.Show answer
It increases. On dark bark the light moths are easy to see and get eaten. Dark moths are camouflaged, so more survive and reproduce, and they pass on the gene for dark colour to their young. Each generation has a higher proportion of dark moths.
Q3Did any moth change colour during its life to match the bark?Show answer
No. Each moth keeps the colour it inherited. Only the proportion of colours in the population changes, because some moths survive and breed more than others. Individuals do not adapt; populations evolve.
Q4Set "Dark moths at start" to 0% and keep hunting on sooty bark. Where do dark moths come from?Show answer
From mutation. In this simulation each young moth has a 2% chance of being a different colour from its parent. A random change in a gene creates new variation. Natural selection then decides whether that variation spreads.
Q5Why might your results look different from your partner's, even in the same forest?Show answer
Chance plays a part. With only 24 moths, a few lucky or unlucky catches change the numbers a lot. Repeating the experiment, using more generations, or comparing class results gives a more reliable pattern. The robot bird shows the overall trend.
Q6After many sooty generations, switch back to clean bark. Predict what happens, then test it.Show answer
The percentage of dark moths falls again, because dark moths now stand out and are eaten more often. This matches what happened in Britain after the Clean Air Act of 1956.

Suggested run for a lesson

1. Hunt 3 generations on clean bark. 2. Switch to sooty bark and hunt 3 to 4 more. 3. Use the robot bird for 10 generations to see the long-term trend. 4. Switch back to clean bark and run the robot bird again. Students sketch the graph and label where the forest changed.
